# Editorial rules

These rules govern every chapter. The governing principle: fill the chapters as a connected argument in which each new concept resolves a problem the preceding chapters could not yet handle. Every chapter must contribute an economic mechanism, a mathematical result, an empirical distinction, or a necessary qualification to an earlier claim. A chapter that only redescribes the programme in its own vocabulary must be rewritten around a concrete problem.

## Structure of a chapter

Give each chapter a substantial explanatory argument before the general formalism. Open with a situation in which a decision must be made and explain what makes it difficult. Introduce the participants, their information, objectives, and constraints. Develop the mechanism in ordinary language, calculate a small example, then state the abstraction the example motivates. After the formal result, return to the original decision and say exactly what changed.

Scale: roughly fifteen to twenty-five substantive pages per chapter, shorter where warranted. Length comes from mechanisms, derivations, comparisons, and exercises, not from restatement. Definitions appear when they become necessary. Every important definition is followed by an example and a boundary case showing what it excludes.

## Three levels of claim

1. A **definition** establishes how a term is used.
2. A **theorem** establishes a consequence of assumptions.
3. An **empirical claim** concerns an actual population, institution, or process and needs evidence appropriate to that claim.

When introducing an original model, state why its assumptions are useful and what observations could make them inappropriate. When an argument is incomplete, call it a conjecture or research question, not a theorem.

## Formal results

For each: hypotheses, which assumptions carry the conclusion, which simplify the proof, which are likely to fail in applications; a counterexample when removing an important assumption changes the result; the proof; then the economic interpretation in a separate paragraph so the reader sees where mathematics ends and interpretation begins.

## Parts I to III: decisions and trajectories

Explain why a continuation description adds something over familiar descriptions. Compare two situations with the same holdings but different outstanding obligations, or the same output but different maintenance requirements. Chapter 4 calculates the cost of establishing access before the benefit of selecting. Chapter 5 confronts conflicts among people's continuations, including cases where protecting one person's security restricts another's actions. Chapter 9 needs a complete investment problem with uncertainty revealed at a stated date, an explicit option to decline, and consistent discounting. Chapters 11 to 15 convert promises into obligations with deadlines and discharge conditions and compute when they remain jointly feasible. Restoration needs an identified payer, a feasible schedule, and access to resources.

## Part IV: valuation, accounting, implementation

Chapter 16 starts from concrete incomparable outcomes before partial orders and multi-utility representations. Chapter 17 derives a scarcity multiplier from a small allocation problem, verifies strong duality, and only then asks whether it coordinates decentralized choices; distinguish supporting prices, equilibrium prices, sensitivity derivatives, and sufficient statistics. Chapters 18 to 20 reconcile the housing accounts with physical condition and outstanding duties: show a balanced ledger, a maintenance backlog, and a restoration schedule side by side, say what each establishes and omits, and do not assign money values to omitted quantities merely to get one total. If monetizing, say whose valuation it is and how obtained.

## Part V: cognition and competence

Give context reconstruction, search, supervision, training, error correction, and intervention explicit resource requirements. Specify how competence changes with practice and disuse, how it affects response time, and how response time affects safety. Derive the practice expenditure needed to hold a chosen operating margin. Compare automation policies over a horizon long enough for competence erosion to matter. The verification chapter includes a small executable check that succeeds while a relevant economic property fails, explains the mismatch between specification and likely inference, then repairs the specification or the interpretation.

## Parts VI to VIII: their own economic models

These need more than applications of the viability operator. Property: control rights and enforcement. Contracts: participation constraints, outside options, information, remedies. Bargaining: a stated procedure or solution concept. Platforms: user responses to fees and quality. Commons: access, replenishment, monitoring, sanctions. Chapter 29 develops P28 fully and Chapters 19 or 37 develop P29 fully. Ecology: regeneration rates, extraction, thresholds, uncertainty. Care: distinguish the recipient's wellbeing from the services their activity supplies, and model the caregiver's constraints. Development: the distribution of access to newly established opportunities. Chapter 40 requires a feasible transition path with financing, temporary burdens, and corrective decisions when milestones are missed.

## Established economics as comparison

Each part explains its relation to the relevant literature (capability approaches, irreversible investment, institutional economics, incomplete contracts, maintenance optimization, common-resource governance, ecological economics). Cite primary sources for claims actually borrowed. Identify where the book reproduces a familiar result in new notation, where it extends one, and where it proposes a new interpretation. A benchmark chapter or extended appendix derives familiar models under explicit specializations: recover a standard dynamic programme first. Competitive equilibrium or a growth model needs its own preferences, technologies, and market structure; slack viability constraints and compressed history alone do not supply them.

## Part IX: rigorous consolidation

Fix theorem statements before polishing commentary. Preserve stable P-labels. Add precise definitions for every operator and comparison relation and make dependencies explicit. Separate finite, countable, and general-state results. State choice, measurable selection, compactness, attainment, or observation assumptions at the point of use.

## Parts X to XII: answers to questions left by the economic text

Category theory: construct the category carefully, prove closure or quotient properties, work through the housing interfaces, and give actual functors with verified laws. Type theory: an explicit judgment system, an execution semantics, separate preservation and progress arguments, a commitment that is expressible but cannot execute, the stronger premise or environmental assumption that excludes it, and what remains trusted outside the system. Sheaf theory: a concrete base of contexts, sections, and restriction maps; prove the sheaf property for at least one record system and exhibit a presheaf that fails gluing; separate compatible descriptions from jointly feasible plans; develop graph price potentials completely before torsors; every cohomological claim names the coefficient structure, cocycle, equivalence relation, and the economic meaning of trivialization.

## Closing every chapter

State what has been established, the decision now enabled, the assumptions still required, and the unresolved question passed on. Exercises include a calculation, a proof or counterexample, and an empirical or institutional application where appropriate, with worked solutions in `solutions/`. In revision, remove passages that repeat the programme without advancing it.

## Prose style

Long academic paragraphs, not strings of short clipped sentences. Avoid em dashes entirely. No first-person autobiographical framing. No invented probabilities, Bayes factors, or scores. Titles: not phrased as questions, avoid "critique", avoid leaning on the "X Before Y" pattern.
