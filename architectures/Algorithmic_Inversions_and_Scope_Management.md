# Algorithmic Inversions and Scope Management

## A Governance Evaluation Framework for Platform Economics and AI Infrastructure

## 1. Executive Assessment and Conceptual Architecture: Economic Inversion and Modular Governance

### Context and Strategic Importance

Modern platform governance routinely falters when evaluating systemic failure because institutional auditors treat economic inversions, including vanity-publishing structures, pay-to-try business models, and non-contingent advertising pipelines, as isolated quality-control anomalies or marginal consumer-protection issues. In reality, these phenomena represent fundamental structural inversions of core market incentives. When a platform reorganizes its business model so that revenue is extracted from participants seeking access to an opportunity rather than from external audiences evaluating the outcome, the institutional mandate shifts from curatorial filtering to volume-based onboarding. To diagnose and mitigate the governance risks inherent in these systems, platform strategists must combine modern economic critique with principles of organizational modularity and hierarchical control. This framework provides an auditing architecture designed to evaluate structural inversions across platform economics, generative artificial intelligence feedback loops, and digital discovery engines.

### Defining the Economic Inversion

In conventional publishing and platform-distribution models, capital risk and institutional authority are bound together. A publisher acts as an investor, curator, and distributor, deploying its own capital to select, edit, produce, and distribute work (Thompson, 2012). Because revenue flows downstream from readers or consumers, the platform's financial viability depends directly on public reception and audience demand. This economic constraint aligns institutional self-interest with public value and makes selective gating an economic necessity.

An economic inversion occurs when the participant or creator replaces the consumer as the platform's primary revenue source. Under this inverted logic, the platform collects fees directly from participants at entry, charging for production packages, distribution listings, tool access, or promotional reach, regardless of whether an audience ever materializes. This structural shift decouples selection from market demand.

**Decoupled selection** means that rejection becomes economically counterproductive because declining a submission means forfeiting a paying customer. Curatorial selection is consequently replaced by high-volume service execution. **Risk relocation** transfers capital risk from the institution to the participant, who pays upfront for the experience of crossing an institutional threshold while the platform captures financial returns at entry. **Monetization of opportunity** replaces verified outcomes with access to an imagined opportunity, selling symbolic tokens of success, including ISBNs, retail listings, and professional dashboards, to individuals whose aspiration serves as the primary operational input.

### Subsidized Patronage vs. Inverted Vanity Models

Rigorous platform audits must distinguish inverted economic models from legitimate third-party subsidized or patron-backed infrastructure. The historical trajectory of the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* illustrates this boundary. Before 1752, the journal was financed directly by individual secretaries, including Henry Oldenburg and Hans Sloane, or by external patrons operating as financial sponsors. From 1752 onward, the Royal Society absorbed publication costs directly, operating the journal as a subsidized institutional service for centuries before publishing surpluses emerged in the late twentieth century.

Third-party patronage, grant funding, open-access subsidies, and institutional sponsorship do not constitute economic inversion. In a subsidized model, capital is provided by an external third party to facilitate dissemination without extracting profit from the participant's desire for recognition. Economic inversion begins specifically when the platform extracts revenue directly from the participant while presenting the transaction as selective institutional endorsement. The diagnostic marker is not the presence of external capital, but the mimicry of curatorial authority used to extract fees from those seeking entry.

### The Modularity and Governance Imperative

To evaluate and contain failures caused by economic inversion, platform architecture must be analyzed through organizational modularity and hierarchical control. As established in classical systems engineering (Parnas, 1972; Simon, 1962), complex systems remain manageable by partitioning functions into bounded modules concealed behind stable interfaces. This design contains internal complexity, reduces the cognitive cost of learning, and prevents local modifications from triggering systemic failure.

| Architectural Layer | Function | Boundary Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Hierarchical governance layer** | Preserves system objectives, scope, evaluative rules, and long-horizon policy. | Local revenue signals may inform review but cannot redefine system objectives directly. |
| **Bounded module A** | Performs a local task or execution function while hiding internal complexity. | Interacts with the rest of the system only through a stable interface. |
| **Bounded module B** | Performs another local task or execution function under the same governance layer. | Cannot acquire authority over other modules merely through local success or financial yield. |

Modularity alone is insufficient when higher-level control fails. In human organizations, algorithmic systems, and cognitive architectures, conceptually mirrored in Plato's image of the charioteer governing drives of unequal strength in the *Phaedrus*, hierarchical governance prevents vivid, local, and short-term signals from capturing the whole system. When an entry-fee platform operates without higher-level governance heuristics, local financial optimization, such as maximizing entry-fee volume or advertising impressions, captures the platform's discovery and verification functions. Scope-preserving governance requires promotional and financial signals to remain contained within their modules so that they cannot corrupt system-wide ranking, verification, or enforcement.

This architectural foundation permits examination of the granular audit mechanisms needed to evaluate entry-fee platform economics and the exploitation of user aspiration under market oversupply.

## 2. Audit Framework 1: Structural Inversions and Entry-Fee Platform Economics

### Context and Strategic Importance

Auditing entry-fee pricing models requires evaluating how platforms operate when structural conditions render baseline user failure statistically inevitable. When a platform positions itself as a mandatory gatekeeper under conditions of extreme market oversupply, its financial survival can become fully compatible with, and indifferent to, the systematic failure of individual participants.

### Auditing Participation as Product and Chokepoint Dynamics

Platforms that occupy critical chokepoints, meaning mediated bottlenecks connecting creators, developers, or professionals to prospective audiences (Doctorow and Giblin, 2022; Srnicek, 2017), derive systemic power from market oversupply. Whether mediating access to book distribution, application marketplaces, professional visibility, or digital infrastructure, the baseline condition is that aspiring participants vastly outnumber successful outcomes. Individual failure is therefore the statistical norm.

When platforms collect non-contingent fees at entry, they monetize the attempt rather than the result. Three operational dynamics follow. **Latent participation** arises because disengagement appears to foreclose the possibility of success, causing participants to maintain subscriptions, profiles, and partially active projects even without proportionate outcomes. **Optimization of display proxies** occurs when platforms substitute quantifiable activity metrics, including tokens consumed, interactions logged, and visibility scores, for direct outcomes, eventually turning representations of work into optimization targets. **Agent-based workflow expansion** occurs when continuous automated workflows draw ever more context and activity into the platform while improving execution inside the system without improving the real-world viability of the underlying project.

| Platform Structural Mechanic | Platform Revenue Effect | Participant Risk Profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Entry-fee collection** | Decouples revenue from user outcomes and capitalizes on the volume of attempts (Eisenmann et al., 2006). | The participant absorbs upfront financial and operational risk while success remains statistically rare. |
| **Shared-infrastructure amortization** | Produces high-margin scale by spreading fixed costs across a large participant pool. | The participant receives standardized workflows while paying for the appearance of bespoke support. |
| **Metric optimization through display proxies** | Sustains interaction, subscription revenue, and retention. | Capital and labor are redirected toward internal platform signals instead of external outcomes. |
| **Agentic workflow expansion** | Enlarges system boundaries, user lock-in, tool consumption, and pressure to upgrade. | Reliance on platform execution tools increases without a corresponding change in external viability. |

### Formalizing Non-Contingent Monetization

Governance audits must classify the transaction mechanics binding platforms, sellers, and buyers.

> **Definition 1: Outcome-contingent vs. non-contingent payment.** A payment made for distributing, enabling, or purchasing an offer or promise $m$ is non-contingent on outcome when its magnitude is determined entirely by exposure, click, purchase, or upfront subscription events, and no term of the transaction makes payment depend on the realized outcome $Y$, such as income earned, audience reached, or skill acquired. An outcome-contingent payment explicitly conditions platform compensation on verification of $Y$.

### The Governance Consequence and the Sensitivity of Indirect Constraints

Non-contingent payment eliminates the platform's direct economic incentive to perform entry-level quality control or curatorial filtering. Because compensation is realized before $Y$ is observed, revenue remains indifferent to whether $m$ represents a viable opportunity or an unfulfillable promise.

Indirect market constraints, including refunds, chargebacks, litigation risk, long-term damage to brand equity, platform-safety enforcement, and user attrition, can theoretically reconnect platform revenue to participant outcomes. The attenuation of extraction can be represented as follows:

> **Remark 1: Sensitivity threshold of indirect constraints.** Non-contingency at initial payment does not imply complete independence over time when refunds $\delta_r$, chargebacks $\delta_c$, enforcement $\delta_e$, or attrition $\delta_a$ penalize failed outcomes $Y=0$. The effective extraction rate is

$$
R_{\mathrm{eff}} = m - \sum_{k\in\{r,c,e,a\}}\delta_k(Y=0).
$$

In markets characterized by continuous user acquisition, extreme oversupply, low transaction values relative to enforcement costs, and disposable platform identities, the attenuation terms may approach zero:

$$
\sum_k \delta_k \longrightarrow 0.
$$

In this regime, indirect constraints cease to bind effectively, leaving platform revenue insulated from user failure. Internal structural firewalls then become the principal mechanism of risk containment.

This extraction of upfront fees from hopeful participants has an operational analogue in generative AI ecosystems, where platforms may extract valuable labor from subscription-paying users under the guise of service delivery.

## 3. Audit Framework 2: Exploitation of User Labor in Generative AI Feedback Loops

### Context and Strategic Importance

A critical governance risk in subscription-based generative AI architecture is the convergence of paying customer and uncompensated training annotator. Auditing these feedback loops requires determining whether user-interaction data is transparently exchanged for software functionality or covertly harvested to offset the capital costs of collecting human preference data.

### Evaluating the Subscriber-Contributor Collapse

In traditional software engineering, the cost of product improvement flows outward from the enterprise. Firms deploy capital to hire engineers, testers, and external data-annotation contractors. Building and refining large generative models requires extensive human judgment, including reinforcement learning from human feedback, response ranking, edge-case identification, and failure-mode correction. Historically, this preference data has been collected through specialized, frequently low-wage annotation pipelines (Gray and Suri, 2019; Ouyang et al., 2022).

| Pipeline | Economic Flow |
|---|---|
| **Traditional software** | Enterprise capital flows to paid engineers or contractors, whose labor produces software refinement. |
| **Inverted generative AI** | A paying subscriber provides both a subscription fee and correction or preference data, which may then contribute to model improvement. |

Generative AI subscriptions create a structural inversion when paying users continuously supply preference judgments through ordinary use. **Reprompting and refinement** after an unsatisfactory response reveal failure modes. **Inline corrections** and choices among alternatives can create preference-comparison data resembling paid annotation. **Labor substitution** occurs when these interactions are captured so that paying customers function as uncompensated annotators refining a commercial asset.

### Comparative Assessment of Consumer AI Data Policies

Whether user-data harvesting constitutes fair exchange or hidden extraction depends upon account defaults, opt-out friction, retention periods, and plan tiers.

| AI Platform Tier or Service | Default Training Policy | Data-Retention Parameters | Governance Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **OpenAI ChatGPT consumer service** | Opt-out model: conversations may be eligible for model training unless disabled through data controls. | Subject to standard system logs; opting out prevents use of conversations for general model training. | Conditional exchange: user controls exist, but no direct financial offset is attached to contribution. |
| **Anthropic Claude consumer plans: Free, Pro, and Max** | Documented 2025 policy used an explicit choice asking whether chats and coding sessions could be used for training. | Five-year retention when the user opts in; thirty-day retention when the user declines. | Transparent choice, although the longer opt-in period requires clear consent and retention disclosure. |
| **Enterprise and commercial tiers across the industry** | Contractual exclusion from training by default under enterprise privacy terms. | Governed by enterprise retention and compliance agreements. | Standard commercial exchange in which the subscription explicitly purchases stricter data privacy. |

### Governance Criteria for Data Harvesting

A conditional three-part test determines whether data capture constitutes hidden extraction. **Disclosure transparency** asks whether conversion of user interactions into model-training data is presented as an unambiguous choice at onboarding. **Pricing reciprocity** asks whether data sharing is compensated through discounted subscription prices or expanded compute access relative to non-sharing tiers. **Data-control autonomy** asks whether users can opt out without losing primary software capabilities or facing asymmetric retention penalties.

Unchecked user-labor extraction and entry-fee monetization frequently intersect with advertising-funded discovery engines, where non-contingent monetization produces acute conflicts of interest in distribution.

## 4. Audit Framework 3: The Discovery Conflict and Non-Contingent Advertising Monetization

### Context and Strategic Importance

Systemic governance risks peak when search and discovery platforms rely on non-contingent advertising revenue generated by high-uncertainty or speculative promises. When the platform providing discovery is compensated for distribution rather than transaction validity, its function as an objective discovery instrument is compromised.

### Deconstructing the Discovery-Conflict Gradient

The conflict between advertising monetization and discovery relevance was identified by Brin and Page (1998), who observed that advertising-supported search engines are structurally biased toward paid advertisers over organic relevance. The conflict is a gradient rather than a binary condition.

| Conflict Position | Typical Query or Offer | Relationship between Paid Placement and User Utility |
|---|---|---|
| **Low severity: coincident interests** | Standard commercial searches for identifiable retail goods. | Paid placement may closely match user intent, allowing advertising and organic utility to overlap substantially. |
| **High severity: imagined opportunity** | AI passive-income schemes, automated ecommerce stores, or turnkey business courses. | High entry fees, unverifiable claims, and non-contingent advertising revenue separate platform compensation from user outcomes. |

Three structural factors maximize the conflict. **Upfront-fee asymmetry** occurs when the seller's entry fee greatly exceeds the marginal cost of advertising reach. **Information asymmetry** prevents prospective buyers from evaluating validity or earning potential before purchase. **Non-contingent advertising scale** allows platform revenue $m$ to grow with impressions or clicks regardless of whether buyers achieve positive outcomes $Y$.

### Evidentiary Tiering and Regulatory Case Synthesis

Auditors should distinguish four evidentiary tiers.

| Tier | Evidence Class | Principal Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Regulatory actions | Public filings and consent orders establishing alleged or adjudicated conduct, quantified losses, and legal findings. |
| **2** | Internal platform documents | Evidence bearing on corporate knowledge, enforcement tradeoffs, scam volume, and revenue-linked guardrails. |
| **3** | Complaint and refund data | Measures of dissatisfaction, reported loss, chargebacks, and dispute trends. |
| **4** | Transparency tools | Public advertising libraries documenting circulation, targeting parameters, and duration. |

### Regulatory Action Synthesis: FTC Deceptive AI Enforcement

Recent Federal Trade Commission actions illustrate how alleged imagined-opportunity schemes circulated across major platform advertising networks.

| Defendant or Scheme | Named Platform Channels | Alleged Consumer Losses or Claims | Structural Inversion Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ascend Ecom** (*FTC v. Ascend Ecom*, September 2024) | Instagram, Facebook, X, YouTube, and TikTok | At least $25 million in alleged losses; passive income promised through AI ecommerce tools. | Turnkey fees collected before store profitability was established. |
| **Ecommerce Empire Builders** (September 2024) | Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and email | Unsubstantiated claims of $10,000 per month through AI-based models. | Non-contingent course and software-access fees scaled through social advertising. |
| **FBA Machine** (September 2024) | Instagram, Facebook, YouTube, and TikTok | At least $15.9 million in alleged losses; guarantees associated with AI pricing tools. | Automated store-management fees decoupled from realized Amazon sales. |
| **Click Profit** (March 2025; final orders August 2025) | Google Search advertisements and TikTok advertisements | At least $14 million in alleged losses; passive income promised from AI storefronts. | Search and video campaigns routed buyers into subscription stores. |
| **Air AI** (August 2025; proposed settlement March 2026) | Social-media advertisements and online videos | Alleged losses of up to $250,000 per consumer; $18 million suspended judgment. | AI sales-agent subscriptions sold through unsubstantiated growth claims. |

### Internal Documents and Precise Evidentiary Provenance

Auditors must preserve the reporting chain through which claims about corporate knowledge enter the public record. The principal reported source for Meta's internal scam-enforcement metrics was a December 2024 internal document first reported by Reuters in November 2025. That reporting was subsequently cited in a November 13, 2025 Consumer Reports letter to the FTC and state attorneys general, as well as a November 22, 2025 letter from Senators Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley to the FTC and SEC.

The document was reported to contain an estimate that roughly 10 percent of Meta's annual revenue, approximately $16 billion, derived from advertisements for scams and banned goods; that approximately 15 billion higher-risk scam-ad impressions were served daily; and that internal policy included a revenue-linked enforcement guardrail limiting automated action when the operational cost exceeded 0.15 percent of company revenue.

Meta disputed this characterization and described the underlying documents as working drafts that did not reflect complete operational realities. The company stated that infrastructure improvements implemented during 2025 produced a 58 percent global reduction in user-reported scam advertisements. Separately, FTC data documented $2.7 billion in consumer losses to social-media fraud between January 2021 and June 2023. Investment scams comprised 20 percent of reports but 53 percent of financial losses (MediaPost, 2023).

### Audit Scope: Proven vs. Unproven Claims

The public record supports the claim that major advertising networks, including Facebook, Instagram, Google Search, TikTok, and YouTube, served as distribution channels for deceptive and high-loss AI income schemes alleged in regulatory actions. Reported internal documents also support scrutiny of revenue-sensitive enforcement thresholds.

Public evidence does not by itself establish that a platform possessed specific internal knowledge about each advertiser at the time its campaign ran, nor does it reveal the exact chronology of internal review decisions for specific accounts. Reconstructing that knowledge requires non-public account histories, risk scores, complaint logs, and enforcement records.

Resolving these conflicts of interest requires strict organizational modularity and scope-preserving boundaries between commercial revenue streams and evaluative judgment.

## 5. Governance Framework: Scope-Preserving Judgment and Modular Boundary Design

### Context and Strategic Importance

Preventing platform failure requires translating organizational modularity into governance design. When local commercial priorities capture institutional evaluation, systems undergo boundary collapse. Governance architecture must enforce the scope of judgment so that evidence, authority, and execution remain structurally distinct.

### Formalizing Judgment Scope and Boundary Violations

Drawing on modular-systems theory (Simon, 1962; Parnas, 1972) and cognitive architecture, platform governance requires precise rules for how assessments interact across organizational boundaries.

> **Definition 1: Scope of an assessment.** The scope of an assessment $a_i$ addressing question $Q_i$ is the set of questions $\{Q_j\}$ upon which $a_i$ is entitled to bear, together with the epistemic manner in which it bears upon them: establishing an answer $I_{\mathrm{establish}}$, providing non-decisive evidence $I_{\mathrm{evidence}}$, or prompting closer scrutiny $I_{\mathrm{scrutiny}}$. Absolute decision authority $A$ remains a separate relation, where $(i,j)\in A$ means that the entity evaluating $a_i$ is formally authorized to execute an answer to $Q_j$.

> **Definition 2: Intelligent organization of judgment.** An organization of platform judgment is intelligent if and only if it satisfies differentiation, scope-preserving communication, provenance preservation, and procedural revisability.

**Differentiation** isolates questions requiring independent evaluation. **Scope-preserving communication** transmits findings across boundaries while restricting their authority to $I_{\mathrm{evidence}}$ or $I_{\mathrm{scrutiny}}$ whenever $I_{\mathrm{establish}}$ is unwarranted. **Provenance preservation** retains assumptions, qualifications, and context as findings travel across interfaces. **Procedural revisability** creates operational channels for altering boundaries when failure occurs while subjecting those changes to anti-rationalization safeguards.

### Three Anti-Rationalization Guardrails for Boundary Revision

An **anomaly or failure trigger** prevents revision for mere convenience and requires documented breakdown, systemic friction, or predictive failure. **Public and consistent application** requires revised boundaries to be explicitly articulated, independently justified, and applied to parallel past and future cases. **Disinterested approval** prevents the entity benefiting from the revision from holding sole authority and requires independent oversight or cross-functional consent.

### The Three Dimensions of Relevance

| Dimension | Function |
|---|---|
| **Evidential relevance, $I_{\mathrm{evidence}}$** | Alters the warranted probability of a proposition. |
| **Practical relevance** | Changes the consequences of a decision regardless of evidential strength. |
| **Legitimate authority, $A$** | Supplies the formal mandate to execute an action or settle a policy. |

Systemic failure occurs when legitimate authority $A$ silently absorbs evidential relevance $I_{\mathrm{evidence}}$, as when a manager overrides safety data on the basis of a financial target or a technical credential is treated as universal social authority.

### Catastrophic Structural Failure Modes

| Failure Mode | Boundary Error | Operational Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Indiscriminate spread** | Local metrics, credentials, or financial interests expand beyond their valid domain. An advertising payment $a_i$, for example, is allowed to determine organic verification as though $Q_j\in I_{\mathrm{establish}}$. | The local signal captures system-wide judgment and technical execution substitutes for ethical or evidential justification. |
| **Impermeable silos** | Rigid insulation prevents critical evidence $I_{\mathrm{evidence}}$ from reaching the decision node $A$. | Modules remain internally orderly while the system loses the capacity for coordinated correction. |

### Engineering and Organizational Case Studies

In the **organizational seating analogy**, a school administrator places bickering students at different positions around a shared round table. Spatial separation contains local friction without excluding either child from the classroom. Total barriers would destroy shared work, while ignoring the friction would permit a local conflict to overwhelm the environment.

In **conversational turn-taking systems** (Bae and Bennett, 2025; Cosper and Pika, 2025; Patamia et al., 2025), treating acoustic silence as absolute decision authority $I_{\mathrm{establish}}$ produces unnatural delays or aggressive interruptions. A scope-preserving multimodal system treats silence as $I_{\mathrm{evidence}}$ alongside prosodic, visual, verbal, and contextual evidence. These signals enter a fusion interface, while decision authority $A$ over floor management remains distinct.

### Decoupling Execution from Goal Selection

Platform governance must preserve a structural separation between operational execution and accountable goal selection.

| Function | Concern | Governing Standard |
|---|---|---|
| **Competent execution** | Operational capacity, task optimization, speed, scaling, and efficiency. | Confirms that a system performs the specified task but cannot establish the social worth or legitimacy of that task. |
| **Accountable goal selection** | Definition of objectives, performance indicators, and operational boundaries. | Requires justification, consultation, and revisability. |

The central mandate is that high execution capacity cannot validate a misallocated goal. **Justification** requires empirical and ethical assumptions to be documented and open to independent audit. **Consultation** gives those bearing operational, financial, or social risks formal channels to contest the objective. **Revisability** permits independent governance nodes that do not profit from continuation to modify or terminate the objective, subject to the anti-rationalization safeguards above.

Translating these definitions into practice requires concrete architectural interventions, including append-only identity namespaces and insulated governance functions.

## 6. Remediation Roadmap: Platform Governance, Identity Infrastructure, and Accountability Rules

### Context and Strategic Importance

Eliminating structural inversion requires actionable policy, software, and governance intervention. Platform operators must move from reactive content moderation toward structural risk management and restore reciprocal risk between platforms and users.

### 6.1 Functional Insulation Mandate

Platforms should establish architectural firewalls between monetization pipelines and four institutional functions.

| Insulated Function | Required Protection |
|---|---|
| **Organic ranking systems** | Search and indexing algorithms calculate relevance independently of advertising bids and fee payment. |
| **Recommendation engines** | Distribution beyond explicit subscriptions remains insulated from commercial-promotion considerations. |
| **Claim verification** | Fact checking, trust evaluation, and claim validation operate without commercial oversight or fee-based exemptions. |
| **Rule enforcement** | Safety audits, policy enforcement, and account-suspension workflows operate independently of revenue tier and advertising spend. |

The firewall does not prohibit commercial placement. It confines paid influence to clearly labeled promotional surfaces and prevents payment from creating authority over organic relevance, verification, or enforcement.

### 6.2 Mitigating Aspirational Exhaust through Identity Infrastructure

Costless profile creation allows operators to deploy disposable identities as algorithmic probes. When creating or abandoning an identity carries negligible cost, thousands of templated profiles can be launched to exploit visibility metrics.

#### Four Diagnostic Hypotheses of Aspirational Exhaust

| Diagnostic Hypothesis | Structural Signature and Behavior | Required Verification Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Probing** | Bursts of templated profiles sample recommendation systems for inexpensive routes to visibility. | Creation-time clustering, coordinated network behavior, and automated duplication analysis. |
| **2. Funnel** | Aspirational bait profiles divert captured attention to paid external schemes. | Outbound-destination analysis, lead-capture tracking, and promotional-boost history. |
| **3. Abandoned personal experiment** | A genuine but abandoned attempt shows isolated creation and no coordinated activity. | Isolated timestamps, uncoordinated network structure, and absence of commercial outbound links. |
| **4. Placeholder** | A passive identity reserves name equity with little or no content. | Content volume, update history, and indicators of domain or name squatting. |

Identity should be redesigned as accountable infrastructure. **Append-only history tracking** records state changes, historical functions, and renamings in immutable logs so that fraudulent repurposing remains visible. **Costed creation and dormancy decay** introduce nominal administrative or verification friction for bulk identity creation and reduce visibility nonlinearly for inert, unverified profiles. **Provenance-based discovery** weights search and recommendation visibility according to verified operational history instead of short-term interaction spikes.

The uncontrolled sequence is costless creation, followed by probing or funnel spam, abandonment without penalty, and systemic noise. The corrective sequence is append-only provenance, costed creation and dormancy decay, provenance-sensitive discovery, and verified legibility.

### 6.3 Comprehensive Governance Audit Matrix

| Governance Area | Observed Structural Risk | Mandated Intervention | Verification and Audit Criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inverted monetization** | Non-contingent fee extraction under high baseline failure. | Outcome-contingent escrow pricing or transparent service-only contracts that renounce curatorial claims. | Audit fee structures and verify clear separation between paid services and selective endorsement. |
| **Feedback-loop extraction** | Paying subscribers are merged with uncompensated preference-data or RLHF contributors. | Explicit opt-in consent and discounted tiers or reciprocal benefits for data contributors. | Review default controls and audit retention schedules, including differences between non-training and opt-in records. |
| **Discovery conflict** | Non-contingent advertising revenue favors high-paying opportunity claims over relevance. | Insulate the four evaluative functions and require substantiation for earnings claims. | Audit advertising-library retention and compare regulatory actions with delivery logs. |
| **Scope-boundary collapse** | Local financial signals or executive authority override empirical safety evidence. | Formalize assessment scope and create independent governance bodies with binding revisability authority. | Inspect decision-provenance logs and verify protected routes for dissent and escalation. |
| **Aspirational exhaust** | Disposable identities probe recommendation systems for reach. | Append-only identity histories, dormancy decay, and provenance-based discovery. | Track creation-to-abandonment ratios and measure the weight assigned to historical provenance. |

## Conclusion: Restoring Reciprocal Risk

The core governance crisis of modern platform economics stems from the erosion of reciprocal risk. When an institution, whether a vanity press, an advertising-funded discovery engine, or a generative AI platform, claims symbolic authority and controls access to opportunity while transferring capital, operational, and evaluative risk onto participants, its functional utility degrades. Under these inverted structures, platforms cease to serve as trusted curators, discovery instruments, or collaborative infrastructures. They instead become mechanisms for extracting value from the human desire to participate in economic and cultural life.

Rebuilding platform governance requires structural realignment. Institutional authority must carry financial and operational risk proportional to its influence. By insulating evaluative functions from commercial pipelines, transforming identity into durable infrastructure, enforcing outcome-linked monetization, and maintaining scope-preserving judgment boundaries, platforms can dismantle extractive cycles of non-contingent monetization. Restoring reciprocal risk is the prerequisite for aligning platform economics with sustainable public value.
